# Solve the skyline first, then everything projects

As of September 2026, the best way to georeference a geotagged iPhone mountain photo is to **treat GPS as nearly fixed and solve for orientation and focal length against a DEM**. You do this with a coarse-to-fine skyline and silhouette alignment, optionally refined by learned photo-to-render feature matching plus PnP. Monocular depth is not the right tool for the pose itself. The core techniques are more than a decade old. Baboud et al. (CVPR 2011), Baatz et al. (ECCV 2012) and Brejcha & Čadík (3DV 2018) reach **about 1–3° mean orientation error when the skyline is clean**. LandscapeAR (ECCV 2020) added learned cross-domain matching against textured DEM renders and ran on an iPhone. Since then there has been almost no mountain-specific progress: I found no 2023–2026 transformer, diffusion or foundation-model paper on mountain photo↔DEM pose. The practical gains now come from general-purpose parts: near-perfect sky segmenters (about 1.4 px skyline error), dense matchers such as RoMa v2, single-image calibration (GeoCalib), and browser renderers that can produce float32 depth and XYZ buffers. The iPhone already writes most of what you need: GPS, true-north heading, a gravity vector (pitch and roll) in the Apple MakerNote, and a 35 mm-equivalent focal length. The catch is that **Safari strips GPS from web uploads by default since iOS 16.4**. No consumer product closes the whole loop of automatic pose, peak labels, trail projection and photo draping with an exposed camera model. PeakVisor and PeakFinder still ask users to drag a silhouette by hand. The rest of this report explains why a fully automatic solver fails on a meaningful share of real photos, which parts belong in the browser and which on a GPU server, and why a "tap two known peaks" fallback is both cheap to build and missing from the market.

## Skyline-to-DEM alignment works to about 2° but fails on messy photos

Classic methods come in two families, and only one of them fits this project. The **"known position, search orientation"** family assumes GPS and field of view (FOV) are known and searches the full rotation space. It correlates image edges or semantic regions against a 360° panorama rendered from the DEM. Baboud et al. modelled edges as direction-aware complex numbers. Their score rewards photo edges that run parallel to rendered silhouettes and penalises edges that cross them, which is effectively a robust oriented chamfer ([GeoPose3K PDF](https://cphoto.fit.vutbr.cz/geoPose3K/data/geoPose3K_submission.pdf)). It needed **2 minutes on a GPU for rotation alone** ([Baatz ECCV'12](https://www.inf.ethz.ch/personal/pomarc/pubs/BaatzECCV12.pdf)). Brejcha & Čadík's 3DV 2018 method correlates DEM-rendered semantic classes (sky, rock, forest, glacier, water) over the rotation group SO(3) with FFTs and fuses the result with Baboud's edge score. On GeoPose3K it raised orientation AUC from **0.52 with edges alone to 0.78 with segments plus edges**. On the Venturi video dataset it cut mean error from 2.88° to **1.92°**. A cheap low-resolution pass takes about 1.5 s per correlation, while full resolution takes 45 s and 12 GB ([3DV 2018 PDF](https://cphoto.fit.vutbr.cz/semantic-orientation/data/semantic_orientation_IEEE.pdf)). A key finding for implementation is that **the information lives in regions, not boundaries**: blurring segment edges by 10–20 px barely changed accuracy (AUC 0.70 → 0.68). Porzi et al., who seeded the search with phone sensors and used a learned contour detector, reached **1.23° mean error, against 9.43° for the raw phone sensors** (same source). NASA Ames gives the simplest formulation to copy: minimise the per-column squared difference between detected and rendered horizon rows over roll, pitch and yaw with Gauss–Newton and a few restarts, dropping columns that are all sky or all ground. That reached **1.51° mean error** ([NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160011500/downloads/20160011500.pdf)).

The second family, **large-area retrieval** (Baatz 2012, Saurer 2016, CrossLocate 2022), matters only when GPS is missing. Baatz placed 88% of queries within 1 km over 40,000 km² of Switzerland, but **49% of images needed human correction of the sky segmentation** ([Baatz ECCV'12](https://www.inf.ethz.ch/personal/pomarc/pubs/BaatzECCV12.pdf)). With automatic DeepLab masks, a re-implementation's orientation AUC fell from 0.84 to 0.40 on CH1 ([3DV 2018 PDF](https://cphoto.fit.vutbr.cz/semantic-orientation/data/semantic_orientation_IEEE.pdf)).

Real-world robustness is the uncomfortable number. On 400 random Flickr Alps photos, Baboud-style alignment registered only **2.75% with its original edges and 9.75% with learned silhouettes**. The raw score also gives no usable confidence, so every result "has to be visually inspected by a human user" ([GeoPose3K PDF](https://cphoto.fit.vutbr.cz/geoPose3K/data/geoPose3K_submission.pdf)). That population is harsher than yours: random internet photos, often without GPS or with bad GPS. PoliMi's PeakLens predecessor, which assumed zero tilt, took FOV from EXIF and searched heading only, **matched 64.2% of geotagged photos** ([arXiv 1508.02959](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1508.02959)). Plan for somewhere in between. The recurring failure modes are consistent across papers: haze and low snow-to-sky contrast, clouds sitting on ridges, foreground trees and people, flat or non-distinctive horizons, and photos pitched down with no sky in frame.

Skyline extraction itself is now close to solved. **YUNet (2025) reports 1.36 px mean skyline error on CH1** ([arXiv 2502.12449](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.12449v1)), and a shallow-learned extractor stays under 4 px on about 90% of GeoPose3K ([arXiv 2107.10997](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.10997)). Pixel IoU is misleading here: older CNNs scored 0.94 pixel accuracy but a **38 px mean horizon error** ([arXiv 1805.08105](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.08105)). Evaluate candidate models on skyline row error, not IoU.

GPS error behaves asymmetrically. At 5–50 m of horizontal error, the rendered horizon of peaks several kilometres away barely moves, so orientation tolerates it. For the same reason, position is nearly unobservable from a distant skyline. LandscapeAR found it **could not improve on positions already within about 200 m** ([ECCV 2020 PDF](https://www.ecva.net/papers/eccv_2020/papers_ECCV/papers/123740290.pdf)). Near ridges are the exception, and there altitude matters most. Tzeng et al. note that on-slope images with nearby ridges are "very sensitive to small changes in location" ([CVPRW 2013](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_cvpr_workshops_2013/W07/papers/Tzeng_User-Driven_Geolocation_of_2013_CVPR_paper.pdf)). The right model is rotation plus focal length, with a strongly regularised position and altitude update. Where EXIF altitude is poor, snap the camera to DEM ground plus about 1.8 m, as Baatz did.

## Learned render-and-match is the upgrade path, but nobody has benchmarked it on mountains

**LandscapeAR (Brejcha et al., ECCV 2020) remains the most relevant learned system.** It renders 12 views of 60° FOV at the GPS position from an orthophoto-textured DEM. It describes keypoints with a descriptor trained on photo-to-terrain correspondences from structure-from-motion, votes for the best renders, lifts render keypoints to 3D through the render's depth map, and runs EPnP + RANSAC with a re-match refinement. On GeoPose3K, **39% of photos land within 1° of rotation error and 60% within 3°**. It beats off-the-shelf HardNet, D2Net and NCNet, and it ran on an iPhone ([ECCV PDF](https://www.ecva.net/papers/eccv_2020/papers_ECCV/papers/123740290.pdf); [GitHub](https://github.com/brejchajan/LandscapeAR)). The authors add a caveat: DEM registration "only makes sense for images that observe a significant amount of content farther away than ca 100 meters."

The obvious 2026 upgrade is to swap the 2020 descriptor for a foundation-backbone matcher. **RoMa v2** (ECCV 2026) is the leading dense matcher. It uses a frozen DINOv3 ViT-L, its code is MIT-licensed, and its backbone carries Meta's DINOv3 licence ([GitHub romav2](https://github.com/Parskatt/romav2); [arXiv 2511.15706](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.15706)). The evidence that such matchers bridge the photo-to-render gap is indirect but encouraging. NASA JPL's Geo-LoFTR, **trained purely on synthetic DTM + orthoimage renders**, localised real Mars descent imagery and improved robustness to illumination by up to 31.8% ([arXiv 2502.09795](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.09795v3)). Historical-photo work also matched across large radiometric gaps with SuperGlue ([MDPI IJGI](https://www.mdpi.com/2220-9964/10/11/748)). An August 2026 cross-view matching survey still lists cross-domain generalisation as an open problem ([arXiv 2608.11093](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11093)), and **I found no benchmark of LightGlue, RoMa or MASt3R on photo-vs-DEM-render matching**. For untextured hillshade or depth renders, edge-domain matching is the better analogue: JPL's salient-edge rendering does 6-DoF localisation against textureless models ([arXiv 2509.25520](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.25520)).

Licences limit what you can use more than accuracy does. MASt3R/DUSt3R are CC BY-NC-SA, the original VGGT-1B and π3 weights are non-commercial, and so are Depth Anything 3 Large/Giant/Nested. Commercially usable options are **RoMa v2 (subject to DINOv3 terms), MapAnything-apache, VGGT-1B-Commercial (gated), DA3 Small/Base/Metric, GeoCalib (Apache-2.0 code, CC BY 4.0 weights) and LightGlue** with a permissively licensed extractor. Original SuperPoint weights carry a restrictive Magic Leap licence ([naver/mast3r](https://github.com/naver/mast3r); [map-anything](https://github.com/facebookresearch/map-anything); [vggt](https://github.com/facebookresearch/vggt); [DA3](https://github.com/ByteDance-Seed/Depth-Anything-3); [GeoCalib](https://github.com/cvg/GeoCalib)). Most research code for this problem is also unlicensed or unverified. Baboud, Baatz/Saurer and PeakLens have no public code, and the LandscapeAR and CrossLocate licence terms are unconfirmed. The core solver is simple enough to write yourself rather than inherit.

The lift-and-solve step is standard. Render a float32 world-XYZ buffer in a camera-local ENU frame. Look up the 3D point under each matched render pixel, rejecting sky and pixels at depth discontinuities, where 1 px equals kilometres. Solve with an unknown-focal minimal solver such as PoseLib's P4Pf inside LO-RANSAC, then re-render and iterate two to four times ([PoseLib](https://github.com/PoseLib/PoseLib)). Because translation along the view axis is poorly conditioned when every point is 5–50 km away, **keep GPS as a strong prior and optimise rotation plus focal length.**

## The iPhone supplies a near-complete pose prior, if you can get it through the browser

iPhones write GPS latitude and longitude, `GPSHPositioningError`, and `GPSImgDirection` referenced to **true north** ([Harley Turan](https://hturan.com/writing/exploring-exif)). Apple MakerNote tag 0x0008, **AccelerationVector**, stores CoreMotion's gravity vector in the phone frame, which gives pitch and roll ([ExifTool Apple tags](https://exiftool.org/TagNames/Apple.html)). One implementation warns that the holding orientation must be inferred from the vector plus the image aspect, not from the Orientation tag ([asterism PR #141](https://github.com/cwage/asterism/pull/141)). Together these give a full rotation prior. Gravity is the strong component and should be accurate to roughly a degree, though I have not verified that. **Heading is the weak one**: expect ±5–15° in mountains from magnetic interference and calibration, and user reports show outright mismatches ([Apple Community](https://discussions.apple.com/thread/2129716)). Treat yaw as a search dimension that the compass only weights. For the intrinsics, use `FocalLengthIn35mmFilm`, converted on the diagonal (f_px ≈ f35 × √(W²+H²) / 43.27). Allow ±2–3% and refine it. iPhones apply **geometric distortion correction by default**, so a pinhole model is a reasonable prior. Ultra-wide edges and Pano-mode cylindrical stitches are exceptions ([Apple forums 775111](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/775111)).

Two practical traps dominate. First, **since iOS 16.4, Safari's `<input type=file>` strips GPS**. iOS 17 added an Options toggle in the photo picker to include location, and WebKit treats the stripping as intended behaviour ([WebKit bug 257534](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=257534)). The app must ask users to enable that toggle, accept Files, share-sheet or desktop uploads, or fall back to a map pin or skyline-based location. Second, **most JS EXIF parsers do not decode Apple MakerNotes**. ExifReader covers only some Canon and Pentax tags, and exifr skips MakerNotes by default but can return the raw bytes ([ExifReader](https://github.com/mattiasw/ExifReader); [exifr](https://www.npmjs.com/package/exifr)). Use exifr for GPS and focal length, plus either a small hand-written parser for the "Apple iOS" IFD or a lazy-loaded ExifTool compiled to WebAssembly ([@uswriting/exiftool](https://www.npmjs.com/package/@uswriting/exiftool)).

Datums line up conveniently. An Apple engineer confirms that **CoreLocation altitude is MSL on EGM2008** ([Apple forums 125281](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/125281)). That is the same vertical datum as **Copernicus GLO-30**, the default global DEM: free Cloud-Optimized GeoTIFFs on AWS with about 4 m global RMSE ([AWS registry](https://registry.opendata.aws/copernicus-dem/)). GLO-30 is an X-band surface model, though, and its **RMSE rises to about 48 m on very steep Alpine slopes** ([ResearchGate Alpine study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/364558357_Global_digital_elevation_models_for_terrain_morphology_analysis_in_mountain_environments_insights_on_Copernicus_GLO-30_and_ALOS_AW3D30_for_a_large_Alpine_area)). Summit positions and near ridges will be visibly off. Where they exist, use national lidar DTMs such as swissALTI3D or 3DEP for the foreground. Their exact terms were not checked in this research. Three more data constraints apply. FABDEM (bare-earth Copernicus) is non-commercial ([Bristol](https://data.bris.ac.uk/data/dataset/s5hqmjcdj8yo2ibzi9b4ew3sn)). Free Terrarium PNG tiles on AWS are the easiest browser source ([tilezen formats](https://github.com/tilezen/joerd/blob/master/docs/formats.md)). **Google Photorealistic 3D Tiles are off-limits**, because their terms forbid elevation measurement and machine interpretation ([Google policies](https://developers.google.com/maps/documentation/tile/policies)).

Geodesy cannot be skipped. Curvature minus refraction drops a target by about **0.068·d² metres (d in km), roughly 680 m at 100 km**, with k ≈ 0.13 ([Engicalchub](https://engicalchub.com/calculators/earth-curvature-refraction-correction-calculator/)). Building terrain in ECEF and then camera-local ENU handles curvature automatically. Refraction then needs an effective-radius correction. For labels, OSM `natural=peak` nodes (with `ele`, `prominence`, `wikidata`) are the open source ([OSM wiki](https://wiki.openstreetmap.org/wiki/Tag:natural=peak)). Snap them to the DEM's local maximum and rank them by prominence.

## Existing products stop at manual silhouette dragging

Every consumer peak app renders a DEM panorama from the viewpoint. After-the-fact photo annotation always relies on the user. **PeakVisor** imports a photo and asks the user to "adjust the rendered 3D terrain panorama to perfectly match horizon" ([PeakVisor](https://peakvisor.com/en/news/identify_mountains_in_photos.html)). **PeakFinder** aligns to the compass and lets the user drag and fine-tune the silhouette. It also exposes an embeddable WebGL panorama API with no photo overlay ([PeakFinder manual](https://www.peakfinder.com/mobile/manual/); [PeakFinder-API](https://github.com/Fabiz/PeakFinder-API)). Only **PeakLens** (Android) advertises CNN skyline matching to correct GPS and compass error ([peaklens.com](https://www.peaklens.com/)). FATMAP shut down on 1 October 2024 ([Strava](https://press.strava.com/articles/fatmap-is-transitioning-to-strava)). None of these apps exposes a camera model, projects trails into the photo, or drapes the photo on terrain.

Commercial visual positioning services do not help. ARCore Geospatial, Apple location anchors and Niantic VPS depend on pre-captured city imagery and are live-session APIs, not post-hoc georeferencers ([Google](https://developers.google.com/ar/develop/java/geospatial/check-vps-availability); [Apple ARGeoAnchor](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/arkit/argeoanchor)). **In the mountains, DEM alignment is the VPS.**

The closest analogue to this project is academic. **Smapshot** is a CesiumJS web globe where volunteers click **at least six** photo↔globe correspondences. A photogrammetric solver then orients the image, and the result is shown as a textured 3D model. The API is open source ([PeerJ preprint](https://peerj.com/preprints/27204/); [smapshot-api](https://github.com/MediaComem/smapshot-api)). Monoplotting tools (WSL, Pic2Map, ImGRAFT, the Mountain Legacy Project's MIAS) follow the same GCP → pose → ray-cast pattern on the desktop ([ImGRAFT](https://gi.copernicus.org/articles/4/23/2015/gi-4-23-2015.html); [Pic2Map](https://github.com/iggroup/pic2map); [MIAS](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/tgis.13229)). Golparvar & Wang automate the ground control points with keypoints between photo and DEM rasters plus SAM ([Computers & Geosciences 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0098300425000652)).

The gap is clear. With GPS and EXIF focal length fixed, only three rotation angles remain. **Two or three tapped peaks determine the pose**, compared with Smapshot's six. No surveyed consumer app offers this.

## Once the pose is solved, the browser does the rest

**three.js with NASA-AMMOS `3d-tiles-renderer`** is the best rendering fit for a TypeScript/Vite/React app. It offers full camera control, a WGS84 ellipsoid, quantized-mesh and XYZ overlays, R3F bindings and reverse-Z depth ([3DTilesRendererJS](https://github.com/NASA-AMMOS/3DTilesRendererJS); [three.js releases](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/releases)). **CesiumJS** is the alternative with the most built in: native ECEF, curvature, log depth and `pickPosition` ([Cesium log depth](https://cesium.com/blog/2018/05/24/logarithmic-depth/)). MapLibre now allows pitch up to 180° and roll, but it gives no access to custom buffers ([MapOptions](https://maplibre.org/maplibre-gl-js/docs/API/type-aliases/MapOptions/)). For the solver, the more deterministic choice is to fetch terrain-RGB tiles within about 200 km and mesh them yourself with Martini or Delatin ([loaders.gl](https://loaders.gl/docs/modules/terrain/api-reference/terrain-loader)). View-dependent level-of-detail streaming can drop exactly the distant detail the skyline needs.

Three engineering choices hold the system together:

- **Camera-relative ENU coordinates.** Transform in float64 on the CPU and upload float32. Raw ECEF in float32 gives only about 0.5 m resolution.
- **Reverse-Z with a float32 depth buffer**, needed for a 1 m to 250 km range ([reverse-Z note](https://x.com/Cody_J_Bennett/status/1836809843509747897)).
- **A multi-render-target pass** that writes ENU XYZ, range, normals and feature IDs into float targets, read back with `readRenderTargetPixelsAsync`.

From those buffers, everything the user asked for is a lookup or a shader:

- **Skyline:** the topmost terrain pixel per column.
- **Inner silhouettes:** Sobel edges on log-range.
- **Peak labels:** project each peak, compare its range with the buffer (plus a ray-march fallback in a Worker), declutter by prominence, and draw as HTML/SVG.
- **Trails and ridgelines:** densified polylines depth-tested against a depth-only terrain pass, with occluded segments shown dashed.
- **Contours:** a `fract`/`fwidth` isoline in the terrain shader.
- **Monoplotting (pixel → terrain):** an O(1) XYZ-buffer read ([WSL Monoplotting](https://www.wsl.ch/en/services-produkte/monoplotting-tool/)).
- **Masking parts of the photo:** by range, feature ID, sky, or a polygon unprojected from the XYZ buffer.

Draping the photo onto terrain is shadow-mapping with a colour texture. Render range from the photo camera. In the viewer's terrain shader, project each fragment into the photo and discard it if it is occluded, lies under the sky mask, or sits outside the frame. Weight the rest by incidence angle to suppress grazing-angle stretching. `three-projected-material` is a starting point but has no occlusion test ([lume/three-projected-material](https://github.com/lume/three-projected-material)), and CesiumJS has no built-in perspective-projection primitive ([Cesium #6531](https://github.com/CesiumGS/cesium/issues/6531)). Gaussian splatting from a single photo adds nothing over a projective texture. Spark.js can render procedural splats ([sparkjs.dev](https://sparkjs.dev/)), but splats need multi-view optimisation to mean anything, so use a textured mesh.

In-browser ML is feasible for sky segmentation (SegFormer-class models via transformers.js or ORT-Web with WebGPU; [transformers.js v3](https://www.huggingface.co/blog/transformersjs-v3)). Check the SegFormer weight licence first. Feature matching is a poor browser fit today: ORT-Web's **WebGPU backend detects keypoints but returns no matches for LightGlue**, and only WASM is correct ([onnxruntime #25227](https://github.com/microsoft/onnxruntime/issues/25227)).

## Monocular depth is a helper signal, not a pose solver

Your preference against depth-first pipelines is well founded. The newest models (Depth Anything 3, MoGe-2/MoGe-3, UniDepthV2, Depth Pro) produce excellent relative geometry and often a FOV estimate ([MoGe-3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.17967); [DA3](https://github.com/ByteDance-Seed/Depth-Anything-3)). However, **no benchmark measures them on kilometre-scale mountain scenes**. Their training distributions are driving, indoor and urban scenes, which implies compressed or saturated range beyond a few hundred metres. That is an inference, not a measured result. One July 2026 paper claims a mountain-specific "TopoDepth" beats UniDepth and Depth Pro on mountains, but it has no code ([LTM arXiv 2607.08711](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.08711v1)). Four jobs suit depth models. First, a **sky mask and occlusion edges**; DA3's Nested series includes sky segmentation, though it is non-commercial. Second, **ordinal depth**: a rank correlation against rendered range works well for scoring competing pose hypotheses. Third, **near-field occluder masking**, so trees, people and huts at 0–500 m are down-weighted in matching and excluded from draping. Fourth, a **FOV sanity check** against EXIF. GeoCalib adds a commercially licensed gravity and focal-length estimate for photos whose MakerNote is missing ([GeoCalib](https://github.com/cvg/GeoCalib)).

## Recommended architecture: an automatic solver with a three-rung manual fallback

| Stage | Where | Method |
|---|---|---|
| Ingest | Browser | exifr for GPS and focal length; Apple MakerNote parser for AccelerationVector; detect stripped GPS and prompt for the iOS Options → Location toggle or a map pin |
| Prior | Browser | Position = GPS (altitude snapped to DEM + 1.8 m if poor); pitch and roll from gravity; yaw from compass as a ±15° weighting; f_px from the 35 mm equivalent ±3% |
| Terrain | Browser (Worker) + CDN/server cache | Terrarium or Copernicus tiles within about 150–200 km, lidar DTM near the camera where available; ENU mesh with curvature and refraction |
| Render | Browser (three.js MRT) | 360° cylindrical horizon profile plus in-frustum XYZ/range/normal/ID buffers |
| Sky and skyline | Browser (ORT-Web/transformers.js) | Small sky segmenter, soft probability, skyline per column |
| Global yaw | Browser | 1-D circular correlation of the photo's elevation-angle profile against the 360° horizon, over a small FOV band |
| Local refinement | Browser (TS/WASM) | Robust Levenberg–Marquardt on yaw, pitch, roll and f with Huber loss on skyline and inner-silhouette chamfer, plus a sky-IoU term; regularised position and altitude |
| Hard cases | Server GPU (FastAPI or serverless) | Render orthophoto-textured and hillshade views; match with RoMa v2; XYZ lift; P4Pf + LO-RANSAC; iterate; GeoCalib when gravity is missing |
| Confidence | Browser | Skyline residual in pixels, inlier count, and agreement between independent cues; route low-confidence results to manual mode |
| Outputs | Browser | Labels, trails, contours, masks, monoplotting, drape; export GeoTIFF or glTF on the server |

This design keeps the common path fully client-side. A geotagged photo with a visible skyline should solve in seconds from rendering and one-dimensional search. The GPU server handles only the cases where the skyline is ambiguous, occluded or absent, and it runs from a TanStack Start server function that also proxies tile keys.

The fallback UX should escalate by degrees of freedom:

1. **Drag the silhouette.** The user adjusts yaw and pitch, and optionally FOV, on the rendered skyline, the same pattern as PeakVisor and PeakFinder. The solver re-snaps after each drag.
2. **Tap two or three known peaks.** Show candidate peak markers from the prior pose. The user taps the image location of each, and a closed-form or LM solve over rotation and f fits them. This rung is the product differentiator.
3. **Full ground-control-point mode.** Paired photo↔map clicks, six or more for full 6-DoF plus focal length, Smapshot-style, for photos without GPS or with badly wrong GPS.

Every manual correction becomes a labelled training and evaluation sample. For a public benchmark, GeoPose3K (about 3,000 photos with full pose, FOV and rendered depth) and CH1/CH2 are available ([GeoPose3K](https://cphoto.fit.vutbr.cz/geoPose3K/); [ETH CVG](https://cvg.ethz.ch/research/mountain_res)). Their data licences were not confirmed.

## Risks and open questions

The biggest product risk is **the GPS-stripping upload path**, which can silently remove the prior the whole design rests on. The biggest technical risk is **automatic success rate on your users' photos**. Published figures range from under 10% (random Flickr photos, no reliable GPS) to about 64% (geotagged, heading-only search). Neither population is yours, so build a 50–100 photo in-house test set early. Several questions are still open:

- Does AccelerationVector survive every capture mode and the picker's HEIC→JPEG conversion?
- How accurate are iPhone heading and altitude in the mountains? No published figures exist.
- How does Copernicus summit error translate into label offsets at 5–20 km?
- Do RoMa v2-class matchers beat skyline alignment on textured-DEM renders at all? This is unbenchmarked.
- What licence terms apply to LandscapeAR, CrossLocate, SegFormer weights, the DINOv3 backbone and the GeoPose3K data?

Refraction on inversions or over snow changes k enough to shift peaks more than 100 km away. Pano-mode images need a cylindrical camera model that no source documents.

## Conclusion

The field's centre of gravity has shifted from inventing mountain-specific algorithms to **assembling general components correctly**. Accurate sky segmentation, a float32 ENU renderer and a small robust optimiser now reproduce what took a GPU-minute and a research lab in 2011, and they fit in a browser tab. Because iPhones record gravity, the unknowns collapse to about one hard dimension (yaw) plus focal length. The research papers solved a harder problem than yours, and your automatic path should be simpler and faster than theirs.

The remaining difficulty is a long tail of confidence and UX problems rather than a missing algorithm. The winning product is the one that knows when it is wrong and makes the fix take two taps. Learned render-and-match, and eventually a mountain-tuned depth model, are best treated as a server-side escalation tier to measure against your own labelled corrections, not as the foundation.
